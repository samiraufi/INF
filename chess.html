Chess is a classic, two-player strategy board game played on an 8×8 grid. Each player commands an army of 16 pieces with the ultimate goal of trapping the opponent's king in an inescapable position known as "checkmate".

History of chess

The history of chess can be traced back nearly 1,500 years to its earliest known predecessor, called chaturanga, in India; its prehistory is the subject of speculation. From India it spread to Persia, where it was modified in terms of shapes and rules and developed into shatranj. Following the Arab invasion and conquest of Persia, chess was taken up by the Muslim world and subsequently spread to Europe via Spain (Al Andalus) and Italy (Emirate of Sicily). The game evolved roughly into its current form by about 1500 CE.

"Romantic chess" was the predominant playing style from the late 18th century to the 1880s. Chess games of this period emphasized quick, tactical maneuvers rather than long-term strategic planning. The Romantic era of play was followed by the Scientific, Hypermodern, and New Dynamism eras. In the second half of the 19th century, modern chess tournament play began, and the first official World Chess Championship was held in 1886. The 20th century saw great leaps forward in chess theory and the establishment of the World Chess Federation. In 1997, an IBM supercomputer beat Garry Kasparov, the then world chess champion, in the famous Deep Blue versus Garry Kasparov match, ushering the game into an era of computer domination. Since then, computer analysis – which originated in the 1970s with the first programmed chess games on the market – has contributed to much of the development in chess theory and has become an important part of preparation in professional human chess. Later developments in the 21st century made the use of computer analysis far surpassing the ability of any human player accessible to the public. Online chess, which first appeared in the mid-1990s, also became popular in the 21st century.

How To Play chess ♟️

Starting chess is all about learning the layout, practicing piece movements, and mastering a few core opening principles. White always makes the first move, and players alternate turns aiming to trap the opponent's king in a checkmate.

ChessBoard

A chessboard is a square grid used to play chess. It features 64 alternating colored squares—32 light and 32 dark—arranged in an \(8 \times 8\) layout.

The Grid System

Files: The vertical columns of squares on the board. There are 8 files, labeled with letters from A to H (from White's left to right).
Ranks: The horizontal rows of squares. There are 8 ranks, numbered 1 to 8 (from White's side to Black's side).
Diagonals: Straight, slanted lines of squares of the same color.
Algebraic Notation: Every individual square has a unique name. It is identified by its intersecting file and rank (e.g., the bottom-left corner is \(A1\), and the center square is \(d4\) or \(e4\)).

Chess Pieces!

A chess set consists of 32 total pieces (16 per player) divided into six unique types, each with its own specific way of moving and capturing.

Pawn: Moves one square forward (or two squares on its initial move). However, it must capture enemy pieces diagonally. If it reaches the opposite side of the board, it is promoted to another piece.

Knight: Moves in an "L-shape"—two squares in one cardinal direction and one square perpendicular. It is the only piece that can jump over other pieces.

Bishop: Moves diagonally in a straight line for any number of empty squares. Each player has two bishops, one that travels exclusively on light squares and one on dark squares.

Rook: Moves horizontally or vertically for any number of empty squares. It is heavily involved in a defensive move called "castling".

Queen: Considered the most powerful piece. It can move any number of empty squares horizontally, vertically, or diagonally.

King: The most important piece in the game. It can move one square in any direction. The goal of the game is to trap the opponent's king (checkmate); if your king is trapped, you lose.

Chess idioms!

Checkmate: A phrase used to declare an absolute, irreversible victory where the opponent's king is cornered with zero legal moves to escape.

Elo: A shorthand term for a player's mathematical skill rating, often used idiomatically as a metric of respect, skill, or status (e.g., "gaining Elo" or "high-Elo behavior").

Blunder: A catastrophic oversight or terrible mistake that immediately throws away a winning position or loses valuable material.

Patzer: A derogatory but common term for a weak, unskilled, or amateurish chess player who routinely misses basic tactical ideas.

Flagging: A fast-paced time-scramble tactic where a player tries to win solely by making rapid moves to force their opponent to run completely out of time on the clock.

Premove: A rapid online gaming mechanic where a player programs their next move while it is still the opponent's turn, taking zero seconds off their own clock.

Tilt: A psychological state where a player gets angry or frustrated after a loss, causing them to play recklessly and spiral into a massive losing streak.

Smothered: A dramatic checkmate delivery where a king is trapped entirely by its own surrounding pieces and cannot move out of check.

Gambit: An opening strategy where a player intentionally gives up a minor pawn or piece early on to gain rapid development and control of the board center.

Fianchetto: A specific strategic development where a bishop is placed on the long diagonal (squares b2, g2, b7, or g7) to exert long-range power.

Zwischenzug: A German chess term used on Chess.com meaning an "in-between move" that interrupts an expected tactical sequence with an immediate threat.

Fork: A tactical move where a single piece attacks two or more of the opponent's high-value pieces at the exact same time.

Pin: A tactical bind where an attacked piece cannot move without exposing a much more valuable piece right behind it to immediate capture.

Skewer: A tactical attack on a high-value piece that forces it to move away, leaving a less valuable piece behind it vulnerable to capture.
